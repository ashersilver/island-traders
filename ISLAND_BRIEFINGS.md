# Island Briefings

These briefings are intended to be shown to a player immediately after they
win an island.  They explain the island in operating terms: what it does, what
must be protected, and how a strong governor usually wins with it.

---

## Agriculture and Fisheries Island

You have bought the island that feeds the archipelago.  Your island produces
**Grain**, **Produce**, and **Fish**.  Other islands need a balanced ration of
Grain, Produce, and Fish (or Meat as a substitute) to keep their populations in
good condition; one unit of each is equivalent to one unit of **Food**.  You can
trade these ingredients separately or pre-package them as Food — whichever
commands the better price.

Your immediate responsibilities are to keep your own population fed, maintain
enough **Farm Machinery** and **Oil** to keep producing, and sell surplus
staples without stripping your own reserves bare.  Agriculture is seasonal:
Spring and Summer bring good Fish runs, while Autumn delivers the strongest
Grain and Produce harvest.  Plan around the calendar rather than treating
every season as identical.

Your workforce matters enormously.  Fields, boats, barns, tractors, and storage do
not produce by themselves.  You begin with a mix of farmers, technicians, and
general labour, but your long game is to recruit and train before labour
becomes the thing that caps output.  **Horticulturalists** improve Produce;
**Veterinarians** improve livestock quality.  Skilled agricultural workers turn
capital into dependable abundance; untrained workers leave expensive machinery
partly idle.

Your priorities:

1. Keep a balanced reserve of staples for your own island.
2. Hold enough Farm Machinery and Oil to avoid production interruptions.
3. Watch sustenance runway across the archipelago; future shortages are
   future customers.
4. Train your workforce before population growth makes your current scale
   inadequate.
5. Expand capacity only when you have the people and inputs to use it.

Best strategy: become the archipelago's reliable food-security partner.  Sell
raw staples early, sell pre-packaged Food when convenience starts to matter,
preserve stock for lean moments, and grow capacity ahead of demand.  Agriculture
wins by being indispensable, not by causing a famine and discovering that
starving customers are poor repeat buyers.

## Mining and Exploration Island

You have bought the island beneath everyone else's economy.  Mining produces
**Ore**, **Metal**, and **Oil**.  Ore is raw industrial feedstock, Metal is the
shaped material Manufacturing truly wants, and Oil keeps farms, fleets, and
factories moving.  If Agriculture feeds the archipelago, Mining gives it bones
and blood.

Your central responsibility is to keep extraction running while deciding how
much value to capture yourself.  Selling raw Ore can bring quick money, but
smelting Metal creates a more valuable industrial product.  Oil is especially
strategic because you both produce it and consume it.  You also need
**Container Freight** and **Mining Equipment** to keep operating; a mine that
cannot ship is just a very expensive hole.

Your workforce determines whether you are merely digging or operating a modern
resource economy.  Miners, technicians, oil specialists, and mechanics all
matter.  Training pays twice here: it raises output and lets you exploit better
capital equipment such as crushers, smelters, rigs, and refineries.

Your priorities:

1. Protect your own Oil supply; do not sell yourself into paralysis.
2. Secure Container Freight and Mining Equipment before shortages stop
   production.
3. Decide when to sell Ore quickly and when to convert toward higher-value
   Metal.
4. Invest in extraction and refining capacity before manufacturers start
   bidding against your own future needs.
5. Build a skilled workforce able to use the island's heavier equipment.

Best strategy: be more than a quarry.  Early sales keep cash flowing, but your
strongest position is as the supplier every industrial island must negotiate
with: Oil for motion, Metal for growth, and enough productive depth that no
single failed trade can choke you.

## Transportation and Logistics Island

You have bought the island that makes an archipelago behave like a single
economy.  Transportation produces **Container Freight** and
**PassengerSeats**.  Container Freight moves goods between islands.
PassengerSeats move people: trainees, nurses, and other workers whose travel
can decide whether another island recovers or stalls.  You are not a side
service.  You are circulation.

Your responsibilities are to keep fleets fuelled, fed, maintained, and
available before demand spikes.  You consume **Oil** and **Food** to operate,
and your fleet carries an ongoing Dollop maintenance burden even after the
ships and planes are bought.  Container Freight will be needed by industrial
players; PassengerSeats become vital whenever training travel or medical
staffing contracts are active — trainees heading to the Education Island and
Doctors or Nurses travelling to host islands both require seats.  If you hoard
seats or fail to list them, the whole human-capital system quietly jams.

Your workforce is the difference between vehicles and a transport network.
Logistics managers, flight crew, seamen, warehouse managers, and especially
mechanics make capacity usable.  Mechanics keep fleet condition from decaying
into outages or lost capacity; because transport demand can spike suddenly,
training ahead of need is especially valuable here.

Your priorities:

1. Keep Oil and Food reserves sufficient for next season's operations.
2. Produce and list both Container Freight and PassengerSeats.
3. Pay attention to fleet maintenance and keep enough Mechanics available.
4. Watch training demand and medical staffing contracts; both are advance signals
   for seat demand that can spike suddenly.
5. Balance cargo capacity with passenger capacity when investing.

Best strategy: become the island that everyone plans around.  Freight gives
you steady industrial relevance; PassengerSeats give you leverage in moments
that matter.  The best Transport governors do not wait for a crisis to discover
they should have bought a passenger liner last year.

## Education, Research and Training Island

You have bought the island that turns population into capability.  Education
produces **Expertise**, **Courses**, and **Patents**.  Expertise is intellectual
capital used by other advanced islands.  Courses are classroom capacity: one
Course can serve a class of up to twelve trainees, and each Course consumes one
Expertise per season to run.  Patents convert research into lasting advantage.

Your responsibilities are wider than ordinary production.  Other governors
need you to train their workforce, and your own island must maintain the
faculty and infrastructure to keep that promise.  You require
**LaboratoryEquipment** to produce, and your training economy must also source
PassengerSeats when students need to travel to you.

**Campus load — Food is on you.**  Visiting trainees live on your campus for
the full duration of their training.  Each one adds to your island's Food
demand for every season they are enrolled.  Watch your **Campus Load** figure
in the sidebar: it shows how many people you are currently housing.  If it
outstrips your Food reserves, your population starts missing meals before the
Farmer ever runs short.  Plan food purchases at least one season ahead of
each new batch of arrivals, not the morning after they show up.

If medical staff are also posted to your campus under a **Staffing Contract**,
they too add to your Food bill for the duration of their stay.

Training fee negotiations can go several rounds.  When you review a request
you can **Approve**, **Counter-offer** (propose a different fee, with a note),
or **Reject**.  The requester can accept your counter-offer, reject it, or
send a counter-counter.  Alert cards appear in each party's sidebar when a
decision is waiting.

Your workforce is your product.  Professors create and govern knowledge;
Instructors make Courses possible.  Education starts with an unusually strong
workforce because every other island's growth eventually depends on yours, but
that is not permission to coast.  If you neglect faculty depth, the whole
archipelago hits a skills ceiling with your name on it.

Your priorities:

1. Maintain enough Expertise to fund Course production.
2. Keep Courses available so training requests do not sit idle.
3. **Track campus load** and stock Food ahead of arrivals — every visiting
   trainee or contracted staff member raises your Food demand.
4. Secure Laboratory Equipment and PassengerSeats before they become
   bottlenecks.
5. Use the counter-offer flow to price training fairly, but do not let
   negotiations stall a student for three seasons while you haggle over 5 Dp.
6. Protect and expand your teaching workforce; it is the archipelago's growth
   engine.

Best strategy: sell progress, not merely Expertise.  If you become the trusted
route by which other islands scale their workforce, they will keep paying you
while their own dependence on your Courses deepens.  The strongest Education
player is both university and venture lab.

## Banking and Insurance Island

You have bought the island that prices time, risk, and recovery.  Banking does
not mint a tradeable commodity.  Its business is service-based: **loans**,
**insurance**, and eventually the wider machinery of finance.  You earn by
putting capital to work and underwriting risks other islands cannot absorb
alone.

Your responsibility is to keep money moving without becoming everyone's
charitable aunt.  Productive borrowers can turn your loans into future
interest.  Risky industries may need life or medical insurance.  Food
shortages, workforce injuries, and capital expansion all create opportunities
for a banker who understands the board better than the borrower does.

Your workforce still matters.  Bankers, analysts, clerks, and underwriting
capacity determine how many financial relationships you can support well.
You also depend on **Expertise**: sophisticated finance is not performed by
vibes, however confidently presented.

Your priorities:

1. Keep capital deployed into borrowers who can repay.
2. Price insurance before disaster, not after it becomes obvious.
3. Watch every island's constraints; their bottlenecks are your deal flow.
4. Maintain Expertise and underwriting capacity.
5. Avoid lending so freely that another island's bad strategy becomes your bad
   balance sheet.

Best strategy: be the best-informed player at the table.  Banking wins by
seeing second-order effects early: who needs machinery, who is exposed to food
shortage, who is one trained worker away from a profitable expansion.  Finance
is most powerful when it looks like help and compounds like ownership.

## ForgeHaven Manufacturing Island

You have bought the island that turns materials into leverage.  Manufacturing
produces the equipment other islands need to scale: **Farm Machinery**,
**Mining Equipment**, **Laboratory Equipment**, **Medical Devices**, and
**Transport Equipment**.  You choose a product line each season, so your
strategy is not merely "produce more" but "produce what the archipelago will
need next."

Your responsibilities are to secure **Metal**, **Oil**, and often
**Container Freight**, then decide which customers deserve your scarce factory
time.  Agriculture without machinery, Mining without equipment, Education
without labs, Medical & Laboratory without devices, and Transport without fleet upgrades
all eventually knock on your door.

Your workforce is the hinge between ambition and delivery.  Engineers,
assembly workers, mechanics, and trained labour determine whether capital
investment becomes actual output.  Manufacturing is especially punishing of
lazy planning: buying inputs for the wrong line or training too late leaves
you with idle capacity while somebody else captures the demand.

Your priorities:

1. Keep Metal, Oil, and Container Freight flowing.
2. Read other islands' constraints before choosing each season's product line.
3. Build the workforce required for the lines you expect to specialise in.
4. Avoid spreading capacity so thinly that you become mediocre at everything.
5. Use your position to sell growth itself, not just objects.

Best strategy: forecast the board one season ahead.  The manufacturer who only
responds to today's shortage is always late; the manufacturer who knows which
island will want tractors, labs, ships, or medical devices next becomes the
architect of everyone else's expansion.

## Medical & Laboratory Island

You have bought the island that keeps productivity from collapsing when human
bodies refuse to behave like tidy economic abstractions.  The island produces
**HealthServices** and **Vaccine**.  It also deploys medical staff — Doctors
and Nurses — to other islands under **Staffing Contracts**.

Your responsibilities are to maintain enough **Expertise** and
**LaboratoryEquipment** to keep producing, protect your own specialist
workforce, and watch the wider archipelago for preventable crises.

**Staffing Contracts** are your second revenue stream.  Any island can use the
**Request Medical Staff** action to hire your Doctors or Nurses on a fixed-term
contract (1–4 seasons).  You review these requests using **Review Staffing
Requests**: approve, counter-offer (propose a different fee), or reject.
Negotiations work the same back-and-forth as training.  Once agreed and tickets
are arranged, your staff depart; they no longer count toward your active
workforce while away and return automatically at the end of the contract.

**What contracts cost you:**
- Staff away = fewer active workers at home → lower production and reduced clinic
  capacity until they return.
- You must have **PassengerSeats** for the round trip (or negotiate for the
  requester to supply them).

**What contracts earn you:**
- The agreed fee (paid at dispatch).
- Goodwill: an island that has your staff will keep paying you for preventive
  services.

**Your starting workforce** is 6 professionals — 2 Doctors + 2 Nurses +
2 Medical Orderlies — all fully trained.  Replacing a departed Doctor means
recruiting and sending someone to university for 3 seasons.  Send staff only
when you have enough left to cover your own production.

Your workforce is unusually specialised from the beginning.  That gives you
high initial competence, but also makes losses or overcommitment painful.

Your priorities:

1. Keep Expertise and Laboratory Equipment available for production.
2. Maintain enough staff at home before accepting staffing contracts.
3. Watch Food-shortage alerts and injury risk elsewhere; both create medical
   demand.
4. Stock or source PassengerSeats before urgent travel is required.
5. Price contracts fairly — enough to compensate for the lost capacity, not
   so much that islands decide to tough out their medical crises alone.
6. Use Vaccines and HealthServices to become preventive infrastructure, not
   merely emergency response.

Best strategy: be the island that sells resilience.  This island wins when
other governors realise that avoiding a productivity collapse is cheaper than
enduring one.  Your strongest deals — and your most profitable staffing
contracts — happen before the patient is already on the floor.
